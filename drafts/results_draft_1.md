2.2. Results
2.2.1 Problem statement and design constraints
	Our goal, ideally, is to identify a minimal antibody microarray panel that can reliably classify SARS-CoV-2 S-protein variants (WT, Beta, Delta, and Omicron). The design of the D4 assay itself imposes a number of practical design constraints (FIG X). First, only one dAb can be feasibly measured at once. As such, models must be evaluated per-dAb, and data cannot be pooled, nor antibody pairs drawn, across different dAbs. Second, while spatial multiplexing allows for straightforward quantification of many array spots, the physical space within the field-deployable cassette, the acquisition area of a POC-compatible fluorescent detector, and the need for replicate cAb spots decreases the number of unique cAbs that can be printed. As such, the optimal assay would be limited to between 3-10 unique cAbs. Moreover, since variant prediction must occur on a single sample at a time (i.e., the current patient sample), we cannot employ any form of batch-level rescaling at deployment. This limits our preprocessing pipeline as well as our ability to calibrate predictions (2.2.12)
	Here, we consider a ‘successful’ antibody pair subset as one that (a) classifies above chance with margin on held-out CV folds, (b) provides a meaningful improvement in Cohen’s K or macro-F1, not just accuracy, over a smaller subset, and (c) yields stable results across reshuffles of the data (multi-seed).
	While the D4 allows us to generate relatively large amounts of screening data across antibody pairs, the multi-class, multi-concentration nature of the problem creates a huge theoretical sample space with which to populate with training examples. Accounting for training samples that sit below the practical limit of quantitation, the overall n is comparatively small. Moreover, the four classes are imbalanced by concentration, since each antibody pair has a unique set of kinetic parameters and therefore distinct binding curves. Combined with the large feature space of almost 30 cAbs per dAb, accuracy-only readouts would provide a poor proxy for whether the model will perform during deployment, since simple changes to the train/test split between seeds can yield significant changes in performance.
	To maximize the likelihood of successful experimental validation, we comprehensively report accuracy, Cohen’s K, macro-F1, AUC-ROC, and log-loss, finding that a combination of these metrics yield more realistic evaluation of the models and can inform the engineering design process.

2.2.2. Data collection and description
	In total, 13 detection antibodies were each screened against 29 unique capture antibodies for a total of over 400 unique dAb-cAb pair readouts. Each dAb is analyzed as an independent dataset of cAb features. For each of these, 8 concentrations (ranging from 0 ng/mL to 500 ng/mL) of spike protein were run in triplicate for each of the four variants, yielding a total of 96 observations per dAb. Evaluated together, numerous sources of variation must be accounted for including the variant label, the concentration (a covariate that mostly modulates magnitude of the signal), replicate (within run technical variations), and, as seen later during data augmentation, batch drift (2.2.11).

2.2.3. Screen results and exploratory data analysis
	First, we analyzed the dynamic range of each cAb:dAb pair, noting that fluorescence intensity increases monotonically with antigen concentration above the LoD, but flattens or becomes dominated by noise below it. As a heuristic filter, we discarded cAb:dAb pairs wherein the change in fluorescence intensity was less than X orders of magnitude across the entire concentration range. This removed pairs with poor dose-response behavior, such as extremely high background or low signal intensity. 
	Next, we investigated the relationship between class separability and choice of dAb. Unsupervised learning and cluster analysis (?) indicates that some dAbs cleanly separate at least one variant across most cAbs (dAb13, dAb17), others show essentially overlapping fluorescence distributions across variants at every concentration (dAb4, dAb11) 
	When decomposed to their principal components, it can be observed that the first two PCs of the standardized per-dAb feature blocks show partial variant clustering for ‘strong’ dAb candidates such as dAb13, whereas ‘weak’ dAbs like dAb4 show much less clear partitioning, generally with minimal obvious structure. Taken together, this analysis indicates that (1) there are certain dAbs that are inherently more amenable to variant discrimination, despite each employing the same cAb array, and (2) that promising dAb feature subsets may contain significant redundant information. Encouraged, we shifted our focus to supervised learning methods to identify which exact pair subsets would be optimal for further characterization and validation.

2.2.4. Data set preprocessing
	Briefly, per-dAb feature subsets were Box-Cox transformed to obtained normal distributions, then centered and scaled for method requiring normalization (LASSO, MARS, PLS). Zero variance features were then removed. To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing parameters are estimated inside the cross-validation folds, so feature ranks reported in 2.2.5 are not confounded by the test fold (expand?). Finally, concentration was retained a label for performance visualization but was not included as a predictor, as this information would be unavailable during a clinical use-case

2.2.5. Cross-validation method and safeguards against overfitting
	In an effort to minimize the impact of overfitting our models to the small sample size during the extensive feature selection process, we instituted a three-level cross validation strategy. First, an outer split was held out for final evaluation of downselected feature sets. Next, an inner repeated k-fold cross validation was employed for hyperparameter and feature-count tuning, with the inner-most layer being used for ranking-stability when applicable (?). By partitioning our data in this way, we can partly safeguard against over-optimistic feature selection results stemming from re-use of the same data for both feature selection and evaluation. Moreover, each feature selection and evaluation run is repeated thrice for the screen-level feature selection and five times for later augmented-data modeling (2.2.10). We report the mean ± SE across these seeds, with large SEs for a dAb serving as a soft indicator of potentially unreliable or stochastic feature selection.

2.2.7. Composite score / Borda list / antibody subset results
	Across six distinct feature selection methods and 13 dAbs, antibody pair subset rankings can vary widely across algorithms, or even seeds with a method. By first normalizing, then aggregating the key rankings from each method, we can identify features that are broadly determined to be important. This helps to avoid cases wherein certain pair subsets are selected based on a single very strong result in one method, despite potentially poor results in other methods. 
	To construct our composite score for each dAb: (1) extract the top-k features from each method; (2) score by inverse importance ranking; (3) sum across all methods/seeds; (iv) report final ranked list with candidate feature subsets.

	In a production context, it is intuitive to question the reliability of injecting additional data, taken from entirely distinct production lots, may adversely impact the robustness of the model. 2.2.11. Batch effects
	When comparing across the three sources of experimental data – original screen data, augmentation batch 1, and augmentation batch 2 – it is clear that incorporation of the original screen data in the form of a Bayesian prior, or simply as an additional set of batch-adjusted samples to combine with the augmentation batches during training, does not yield improved predictive performance. The most obvious reason for this is the difference in assay setup between the screen and the data augmentation, namely, the drastically decreased size of the cAb array in the augmentation batches. More highly multiplexed arrays naturally provide for more sources of variation mainly in the form of cross-reactivity between cAbs. Despite producing seemingly strong predictive models for variant discrimination, this data cannot be meaningfully included in our new, more targeted screen. By contrast, despite the augmentation batches being fabricated from different lots of polymerized slides, printed in different runs, and utilizing different aliquots of the same cAb/dAb, inclusion of the second batch with the first still produces significantly better models compared to only utilizing one batch alone.

2.2.13. Deployment method and considerations
	A number of decisions must be made when deploying the final assay. First, there is a clear degradation of predictive performance at S-protein concentrations less than 1 ng/mL. Consequently, in such cases, even if a positive signal is obtained, a variant determination should not be provided. This concentration threshold can be determined in one of two ways: using a known SARS-CoV-2 N-protein as a proxy for S-protein levels or directly utilizing the assay fluorescence intensity. The former provides the benefit of a lower LoD readout (a product of comparatively higher concentration of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein in clinical samples) and a more robust capacity to diagnose SARS-CoV-2, even without a specific variant classification. The latter, by contrast, alleviates the need to include and optimize the assay for another antibody, albeit at slightly diminished functionality. Furthermore, additional confidence safeguards can be added by including the calibrated class probabilities, not just the argmax. Predictions with confidence below a predefined threshold can be flagged, either as assay failures, potentially emerging variants, or unknown anomalies. 
	Motivated directly by the batch effects noted in section 2.2.11., the results of any new device should be collected and monitored for changes in the distribution of the incoming data and . Naturally, if periodic Batesian updates are applied based on field-collected samples, the prior (i.e., data collected during development and training) decays as field data accumulates. While the intended behavior, incoming updates must be analyzed for potential assay artifacts which could bias the model towards noise and systemic error generated by in-field use rather than true signal. This can likely be mitigated by periodically repeating in-house data augmentation.
